\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter presents the experimental setup and comprehensive results. We begin with frozen-embedding baselines, proceed through architecture and training ablations on a small dataset, and culminate in data scaling experiments that reveal data volume as the dominant factor in classification performance.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

Training data is generated by simulating Illumina short reads (150~bp, HiSeq 2500 error profile) from the HGR-UMGS reference genome database~\cite{Almeida2019} using the ART read simulator~\cite{Huang2012}, following the same protocol as MetaTransformer~\cite{Wichmann2023}. The dataset covers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{120 genera} and \textbf{1,535 species} of human gut microorganisms.
    \item \textbf{258M total reads} across all species.
    \item Highly skewed distribution: the most abundant genus (\textit{Collinsella}) accounts for 22.8\% of reads, while the rarest genera contribute $<$0.06\%.
\end{itemize}

We conduct experiments at three data scales by subsampling from the full dataset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{500K} (imbalanced): The initial small-scale dataset preserving the natural class distribution. Train: 450K, validation: 50K.
    \item \textbf{5M} (balanced): Species-balanced subsample. Each of 1,535 species contributes $\sim$3,257 reads. Train: 4.5M, validation: 500K.
    \item \textbf{50M} (balanced): Species-balanced subsample. Each species contributes $\sim$32,573 reads. Train: 45M, validation: 5M.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reads versus unique reads.} The read counts above (and the data-scaling axis in Figure~\ref{fig:data-scaling}) refer to \emph{training reads including duplicates}, which is the quantity the optimiser actually sees per epoch. The underlying source is heavily duplicated at the sequence level: the full read set contains 258.67M reads but only 42.64M \emph{unique} sequences ($6.07\times$ redundancy), a property of the source simulation rather than of our balancing. Consequently the larger balanced pools contain fewer distinct reads than their nominal size: the 50M pool comprises 17.76M unique reads and the 250M pool 41.94M unique reads---the latter already approaching the 42.64M source ceiling. This duplication structure is one reason data scaling saturates: beyond $\sim$50M reads, additional ``reads'' are increasingly repeats the model has already seen.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All models are evaluated on a held-out validation set using the metrics described in Section~\ref{sec:method-eval}. Unless otherwise noted, results are reported with RC TTA (Eq.~\ref{eq:rc-tta}).

\subsection{Hardware and Training Infrastructure}

All experiments are run on NVIDIA H100 80~GB HBM3 GPUs on the TWCC SLURM cluster. The 48-hour SLURM time limit requires checkpoint-resume support for training runs exceeding this duration.

\label{sec:exp-frozen}

\textbf{Key findings from v1--v3}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The token-level approach with LoRA (v1) matches the frozen mean-pool baseline (51.70\%), validating the architecture but showing that token-level representations alone do not guarantee improvement when the backbone is partially frozen via LoRA.
    \item Class-weighted cross-entropy with label smoothing (v2) catastrophically degrades performance to 27.87\%, a $-23.8$~pp drop. This is caused by the interaction between class weights and label smoothing on a highly imbalanced 120-class problem.
    \item RC augmentation (v3) provides a solid $+2.2$~pp improvement (51.70\% $\to$ 53.92\%), confirming the value of exploiting DNA strand symmetry.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Training Dynamics and Overfitting}
\label{sec:exp-overfit}
The training dynamics reveal severe overfitting (Figure~\ref{fig:training-dynamics}, left): training accuracy climbs to 62.7\% by epoch 35 while validation accuracy saturates near 54\%, and the train--validation gap widens from 0\% to 9\%. That gap indicates the model has sufficient capacity to memorize the training data but fails to generalize, suggesting that 500K reads is insufficient to train a 498M-parameter model, even with only 1.1\% of parameters unfrozen.

\figref{fig:training-dynamics} puts that trajectory beside the 5M one, where the same gap never exceeds 1.3~pp---the transition from data starvation to well-generalised training happens between these two budgets.

\textbf{Logit Adjustment} (v5, v5b): Logit adjustment (Eq.~\ref{eq:logit-adjustment}) with $\tau=1.0$ is too aggressive, dropping accuracy to 35.17\%. A milder $\tau=0.3$ preserves micro accuracy (52.29\%) while reducing F1$=$0 classes from 9 to 5, showing a favorable trade-off between overall and per-class performance.

\textbf{Reduced sequence length} (v4): Reducing \texttt{max\_length} from 128 to 32 tokens slightly reduces accuracy ($-0.07$~pp) but also reduces the overfitting gap (6.96\% $\to$ 6.25\%) and decreases training time.

\textbf{Conclusion}: All training techniques on the 500K dataset yield at most $\pm0.5$~pp improvement in RC TTA accuracy (range: 53.58\%--55.36\%). This plateau strongly suggests that \emph{data volume}, not model architecture or training tricks, is the primary bottleneck.

\section{Data Scaling Experiments}
\label{sec:exp-scaling}

Motivated by the accuracy plateau on 500K data and the comparison with MetaTransformer~\cite{Wichmann2023} (which trained on a large-scale balanced dataset trained on the full HGR-UMGS dataset of 2,505 species with coverage factor~4), we systematically scale training data.

\subsection{NT-Genus-5M: 5M Balanced Reads}

\subsubsection{Setup}

A 5M species-balanced subsample is drawn from the full 258M dataset. Each of 1,535 species contributes $\sim$3,257 reads, yielding near-perfect class balance. The v3 architecture and training configuration are retained (batch size 32, gradient accumulation 2, effective batch size 64), and training proceeds for 30 epochs.

Due to the 48-hour SLURM time limit, training required two jobs: the initial run completed 14 of 20 epochs before timeout, and a resumed run completed 16 additional epochs (epochs 15--30) using the \texttt{--resume} mechanism. A third job performed final evaluation.

The results reveal clear log-linear scaling behaviour:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: Each $10\times$ data increase yields a substantial but diminishing gain in RC TTA accuracy: $+7.76$~pp (500K$\to$5M) and $+4.02$~pp (5M$\to$50M). The sub-linear trend is consistent with logarithmic scaling laws.
    \item \textbf{Macro F1}: Data scaling disproportionately benefits rare classes: $+12.27$~pp at the first step and $+6.85$~pp at the second. At 50M reads, all 120 genera have non-zero F1 for the first time.
    \item \textbf{F1$=$0 classes}: The number of unclassifiable genera drops from 9 to 2 (NT-Genus-5M) and finally to 0 (NT-Genus), meaning every genus in the dataset is correctly predicted for at least some reads.
    \item \textbf{Overfitting}: The train-validation gap shrinks dramatically from 6.25\% (v4) to 1.30\% (NT-Genus-5M) to $<$1\% (NT-Genus), confirming that the model transitions from data starvation to well-generalised training as data volume increases.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Training Dynamics}

At 5M reads the train--validation gap stays within $\pm1.3$~pp for all 30 epochs (Figure~\ref{fig:training-dynamics}, centre), in sharp contrast to the 500K runs. The temporary drop at epoch 15 is the learning-rate scheduler resetting to its warmup phase after the job resumed at the 48-hour limit; the model recovers within 3--4 epochs and surpasses its pre-interruption accuracy.

\subsection{NT-Genus Model: 50M Balanced Reads}
\label{sec:exp-v9}

Building on NT-Genus-5M, we scale training data by another $10\times$ to 50M balanced reads. The batch size is increased from 32 to 256 (with gradient accumulation reduced from 2 to 1, yielding an effective batch size of 256) to improve GPU utilization. The model is initialized from NT-Genus-5M's best checkpoint using \texttt{--init\_from} (transfer learning mode).

\subsubsection{Results}

Scaling from 5M to 50M balanced reads yields a further $+4.02$~pp improvement in RC TTA accuracy (63.05\% $\to$ 67.07\%). Critically, the number of F1$=$0 classes drops to \textbf{zero}, indicating that all 120 genera are now correctly classified in at least some reads.

The marginal return diminishes relative to the previous $10\times$ scaling step: 500K$\to$5M yielded $+7.76$~pp while 5M$\to$50M yields $+4.02$~pp for the same multiplicative data increase, consistent with a logarithmic scaling relationship. This sub-linear trend is expected under log-linear scaling laws and suggests that continued data scaling alone will not close the remaining gap with MetaTransformer.

\paragraph{Training progress and LR restart incidents.}
Training spanned several SLURM jobs because of the 48-hour limit, and before the
checkpoint-resume logic was fixed two restarts reset the cosine scheduler to its
warmup phase, costing 0.54~pp at epoch~7 and 1.39~pp at epoch~11; the model recovered
within 3--5 epochs each time. The cause was \texttt{EarlyStopping.best} being silently
restored as \texttt{None} from an incomplete checkpoint key. From epoch~17 the
scheduler state is restored correctly and the decay continues uninterrupted.

NT-Genus training completed at epoch~30 (April~2026), reaching a forward validation accuracy of \textbf{66.29\%} and RC~TTA accuracy of \textbf{67.07\%} (Figure~\ref{fig:learning-curves}). The learning curve plateaued between epochs~26 and~30 as the cosine schedule reached its minimum, indicating convergence.

\subsection{Data Scaling Analysis}

The data scaling analysis reveals a clear relationship between training data volume and classification performance. The transition from 500K to 5M reads ($10\times$ increase) yields $+7.76$~pp in accuracy and $+12.27$~pp in macro F1. Meanwhile, all training technique ablations on the 500K dataset (v3--v7) produced at most $\pm0.5$~pp variation. This strongly supports the conclusion that \textbf{data volume, not model architecture or training techniques, is the primary determinant of classification accuracy} in this setting.

The comparison with MetaTransformer further reinforces this finding: despite using a much larger backbone (498M vs.\ $\sim$5M encoder parameters; see Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparison} for the full parameter accounting), our model at 5M training reads achieves only 63\% accuracy, while MetaTransformer, trained on the full HGR-UMGS dataset with coverage factor~4, achieves 98.3\% genus recall (98.9\% precision) on its validation set. The gap is attributable primarily to the substantial difference in training data volume.

\figref{fig:data-scaling} plots genus RC TTA accuracy against training data volume on a logarithmic scale, with a log-linear fit to our three data points. The fitted curve projects that closing the gap with MetaTransformer would require training data well beyond our 50M-read scale, consistent with diminishing returns under logarithmic scaling.

\paragraph{Beyond 50M: the 6-mer model saturates, but the 13-mer model does not.}
The projection above tentatively attributes the residual gap to insufficient data. To test this directly, we scaled the balanced subsample by a further $5\times$ to 250M reads and trained two variants on a $64\times$H200 DDP cluster: a warm-started model initialised from the 50M NT-Genus checkpoint (mirroring the $5\text{M}\to 50\text{M}$ transfer) and a from-scratch model. Evaluated on an independent, read-disjoint test set under the identical RC-TTA protocol---on which the 50M NT-Genus model reproduces its $67.07\%$ to within $0.05$~pp---the 250M warm-started model attains only \textbf{67.29\%} genus Top-1 (RC~TTA), a $+0.22$~pp change over 50M despite the $5\times$ data increase, while the from-scratch 250M model is in fact lower at $64.84\%$. For the NT-v2 6-mer model, data scaling therefore \emph{saturates} beyond 50M rather than continuing along the log-linear trend.

Crucially, this saturation is a property of the \emph{tokenization}, not of the data or the single-genome-per-species reference. Trained on the \emph{same} reads, MetaTransformer with overlapping 13-mer tokenization continues to improve sharply with scale: on a common clean held-out pool its genus Top-1 rises from 87.5\% at 50M (the 87.42\% of Section~\ref{sec:exp-tokenization}) to \textbf{98.7\%} at 250M---and from 83.0\% to 98.5\% on a stricter ``leftover'' pool constructed to be sequence-ID-disjoint from both training sets, which rules out leakage. The identical $5\times$ data increase that moves the 6-mer model by $+0.22$~pp moves the 13-mer model by ${\sim}+11$~pp. The additional reads are therefore not redundant in any absolute sense: they carry discriminative signal that a non-overlapping 6-mer representation cannot resolve but an overlapping 13-mer one can. What saturates at 50M is the \emph{information ceiling of the 6-mer tokenizer}, not the data.

We therefore conclude that whether additional training data helps is governed by tokenization: the remaining gap to MetaTransformer is \emph{not} closable by additional data within the NT-v2 6-mer framework, whereas an overlapping long-$k$ tokenizer converts the same reads into near-perfect genus assignment. We caution, however, that the 98.7\% figure reflects \emph{near-complete closed-set coverage of the 1{,}535 reference genomes' 13-mer content}---at 250M reads the overlapping 13-mer model approaches a lookup over the known genomes---and does \emph{not} measure generalisation to novel, unseen genomes. The operative ceiling for our setting is thus tokenization (Section~\ref{sec:exp-tokenization}) rather than data volume.

\subsection{Balancing Axis: Species-Balanced vs.\ Genus-Balanced Training}
\label{sec:exp-genus-balance}

The scaling experiments above are species-balanced (each of the 1{,}535 species contributes equally). A natural alternative is to balance at the \emph{genus} level (each of the 120 genera contributes equally), which up-weights species-poor genera. To isolate the effect of the balancing axis alone, we trained two NT-v2 6-mer models on the \emph{same} 17.6M-read budget, differing only in this choice, and evaluated both on a common natural-abundance read pool. Table~\ref{tab:genus-balance} reports the result across three complementary metrics: per-read micro accuracy (the rate a randomly drawn read is classified correctly, the operationally relevant quantity for real samples), per-genus flat accuracy (macro-averaged over the 120 genera, treating each genus equally), and the Pearson correlation of estimated versus true relative abundance on real samples.

Two findings follow. First, genus-balancing is \emph{counterproductive for real metagenomics}: although it lifts the macro/flat accuracy that weights rare genera equally ($33.1\% \to 43.5\%$, $+10.4$~pp), it does so by sacrificing exactly the reads that dominate real samples---per-read accuracy collapses from $60.4\%$ to $37.2\%$ ($-23.2$~pp) and the relative-abundance correlation degrades from $r=0.993$ to $r=0.862$. Because real samples are dominated by abundant genera, the per-read and abundance metrics are the ones that matter operationally, and on both the species-balanced model is decisively better. Second, the per-genus flat accuracy plateaus at $\sim$42--43\% \emph{even when the training distribution is explicitly genus-balanced}. This ceiling is therefore not a fixable artefact of class imbalance; it reflects the intrinsic difficulty of resolving 120 genera from 150~bp reads under 6-mer tokenization---consistent with the train-fit ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:exp-tokenization}.

\section{RC Test-Time Augmentation Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-rc-tta}

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Effect of RC TTA across experiments. RC TTA consistently improves accuracy at the cost of $2\times$ inference time.}
    \label{tab:rc-tta}
    \begin{tabular}{lccr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Experiment} & \textbf{Fwd Only} & \textbf{RC TTA} & \textbf{$\Delta$} \\
        \midrule
        v3 (500K genus) & 53.92\% & 55.36\% & $+1.44$~pp \\
        v4 (500K genus) & 53.75\% & 55.29\% & $+1.54$~pp \\
        v5b (500K, LA $\tau$=0.3) & 52.29\% & 53.58\% & $+1.29$~pp \\
        v7 (500K, RC consist.) & 54.10\% & 55.18\% & $+1.08$~pp \\
        NT-Genus-5M (5M genus) & 62.02\% & 63.05\% & $+1.03$~pp \\
        NT-Genus (50M genus) & 66.29\% & 67.07\% & $+0.78$~pp \\
        NT-Genus (250M genus, warm-start) & 66.61\% & 67.29\% & $+0.68$~pp \\
        KmerFormer (50M genus, 1 layer)   & 53.80\% & 53.88\% & $+0.08$~pp \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

RC TTA provides a consistent $+1.0$ to $+1.5$~pp accuracy improvement across all experiments, regardless of the training configuration or data scale. Several observations merit discussion:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Biological basis}: As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg-rc-symmetry}, a metagenomic read and its reverse complement encode identical biological information. RC TTA exploits this strand symmetry by treating the two orientations as a two-view ensemble of the same underlying genomic entity~\cite{Zhou2022rc}.
    
    \item \textbf{Variance reduction}: The consistent improvement across all settings is explained by standard ensemble theory: averaging two correlated but independently noisy estimates of the same ground-truth label reduces prediction variance, even when both estimates come from the same model.
    
    \item \textbf{Diminishing returns with RC consistency training}: v7 (RC consistency, $+1.08$~pp) shows less benefit than vanilla models, because RC consistency training already encourages $f(s) \approx f(\bar{s})$ during training, leaving less residual strand asymmetry for RC TTA to correct.

    \item \textbf{Scale-dependent diminishing returns}: The RC TTA benefit decreases slightly with data scale: $+1.44$~pp at 500K, $+1.03$~pp at 5M, $+0.78$~pp at 50M, and $+0.68$~pp at 250M (NT-Genus). Larger training sets expose the model to both strands of each locus more often, gradually reducing systematic strand-directional errors---though a non-trivial asymmetry persists even at 50M reads.

    \item \textbf{Near-zero benefit for from-scratch models}: KmerFormer at one layer shows only $+0.08$~pp from RC TTA. Section~\ref{sec:exp-shallow} shows that this holds at every depth, so it is not a capacity effect, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-thirteenmer} locates the mechanism in the vocabulary: a canonical vocabulary makes the augmentation arithmetically a no-op, a non-canonical one does not. RC TTA corrects a strand asymmetry a model has been allowed to acquire, and a model trained from scratch with reverse-complement augmentation does not acquire one.
\end{enumerate}

The $2\times$ inference cost is the primary trade-off. For offline evaluation this is acceptable; for latency-sensitive deployment, RC-equivariant architectures~\cite{Brown2021rc} or RC consistency regularization~\cite{Ma2025rc} may be preferred alternatives.

Despite using a foundation model $100\times$ larger than MetaTransformer, our model (at 50M training reads) substantially underperforms MetaTransformer (trained on the full HGR-UMGS dataset). The key differences are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training data volume}: MetaTransformer uses the full HGR-UMGS dataset with coverage~4 vs. our 50M balanced reads. Given our data scaling results (each $10\times$ data increase yields $+3$--$8$~pp with diminishing returns), this is likely the dominant factor.
    
    \item \textbf{Training paradigm}: MetaTransformer trains all encoder parameters from scratch ($\sim$5M; the embedding table is additional---see parameter-accounting caveat below), while we fine-tune only 5.54M LoRA parameters of a 498M-parameter model. The foundation model's pre-trained representations, while beneficial as a starting point, may not be optimally suited for metagenomic classification without sufficient fine-tuning data.
    
    \item \textbf{Tokenization}: MetaTransformer uses 12-mer tokenization (capturing longer sequence patterns per token), while NT-v2 uses 6-mers. The coarser 12-mer tokenization may be advantageous for species discrimination, as it directly captures longer characteristic motifs.
\end{enumerate}

This comparison is confounded by three simultaneous differences: backbone depth (29 vs.\ 1 layer), pre-training (yes vs.\ no), and tokenization (6-mer vs.\ 12-mer overlapping). Sections~\ref{sec:exp-shallow} and~\ref{sec:exp-tokenization} disentangle them through controlled ablations, each moving one factor inside one codebase. The key findings are that tokenization accounts for the larger share of the gap, that encoder depth accounts for most of the remainder, and that pre-training's own net contribution at this data scale is approximately zero---large only when labelled reads are scarce (Section~\ref{sec:exp-lowdata}).

\paragraph{The ``$\sim$5M parameters'' figure counts only the encoder.}
The $\sim$5M parameter count attributed to MetaTransformer throughout this comparison refers to its \emph{transformer encoder} alone; it omits the token-embedding table, which for large-$k$ tokenization dominates the parameter budget. A learned $k$-mer embedding assigns a distinct vector to every $k$-mer observed in training, so the embedding matrix grows with the realized vocabulary. For the overlapping 13-mer (stride~1) configuration that attains 87.42\% at 50M and 98.7\% at 250M, the realized vocabulary reaches ${\sim}33.5$M distinct $k$-mers; at the embedding width $d_{\text{model}}{=}64$, the embedding matrix alone is ${\sim}33.5\text{M}\times 64 \approx 2.1$ billion parameters---roughly $4\times$ the size of the entire 498M-parameter NT-v2 backbone. The 13-mer model is therefore parameter-light only in \emph{compute} (a single shallow encoder block over short token sequences), while being parameter-\emph{heavy} in \emph{storage} (a multi-billion-entry lookup table). This is intrinsic to the approach: its discriminative power comes precisely from giving each long $k$-mer its own vector, which is also what inflates the table. A fair efficiency comparison must therefore weigh MetaTransformer's small compute footprint against this large embedding-memory cost, rather than citing the ${\sim}5$M encoder figure as the model's total size.

\section{Depth-Controlled Backbone Ablation}
\label{sec:exp-shallow}

Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparison} left three factors entangled: pre-training, encoder depth, and tokenization. This section removes the first two by fixing the tokenizer at NT-v2's non-overlapping 6-mer and varying only the encoder that sits on top of it.

The from-scratch arms are all instances of \textbf{KmerFormer} (Section~\ref{sec:method-shallow}), the $k$-mer transformer in which the tokenizer, the stride, the vocabulary and the depth are free parameters rather than inherited from a pre-training run. The single-layer arm reported in earlier sections is KmerFormer at one layer; the deeper arms introduced below are the same code and the same training recipe with only the depth changed.

\subsection{Why the Single-Layer Control Is Not Sufficient}

Our first version of this ablation compared the 29-layer pre-trained NT-v2 backbone against a \emph{single-layer} KmerFormer trained from scratch, holding the tokenizer, the training reads, the label space and the classification head identical (Table~\ref{tab:shallow-design}). At 50M balanced reads that comparison gives 67.07\% against 53.88\% under RC TTA---a gap of 13.19~pp---and it is tempting to read the gap as the value of pre-training.

It cannot be read that way, because the two arms also differ in depth: 29 layers against one. Depth is a free parameter of KmerFormer and a fixed one of NT-v2, so an ablation that moves it alongside pre-training cannot attribute the outcome to either. The rest of this section resolves the confound by sweeping depth with everything else held constant. The attribution changes, and so does the conclusion drawn from it.

\textbf{Depth alone accounts for 15.60~pp.} Going from 1 to 29 layers moves accuracy from 53.92\% to 69.52\% with no pre-training anywhere in the picture. That is larger than the 13.19~pp originally attributed to pre-training, and it is obtained by changing the one factor the original ablation failed to hold fixed. Returns diminish but never turn negative: $+11.52$, $+2.50$ and $+1.58$~pp at each step.

\textbf{At comparable depth, pre-training's net contribution at 50M is approximately zero.} A 3.88M-parameter 16-layer KmerFormer reaches 67.94\%, already $+0.86$~pp above NT-Genus; at NT-v2's own depth of 29 layers, a 6.45M-parameter model reaches 69.52\%, $+2.44$~pp above it with $77\times$ fewer parameters and no pre-training run. The correct statement is therefore not that pre-training is worth $+13.19$~pp under fixed tokenization, but that \emph{at 50M labelled reads and matched depth its net contribution is slightly negative}. The earlier figure conflated pre-training with a 1-versus-29-layer depth difference.

\textbf{The macro-averaged metrics move the same way.} Balanced accuracy rises from 22.0\% to 46.2\% across the depth series and NT-Genus sits at 39.8\%, below both the 16- and 29-layer arms. The rare-genus deficit previously attributed to the shallow model's limited capacity is likewise a depth effect: it disappears once depth is supplied.

A LoRA asymmetry remains and is worth stating. NT-v2 is adapted through 5.54M LoRA parameters, roughly 1\% of its weights, whereas every KmerFormer arm is trained end to end. A full fine-tune of NT-v2 might score higher than 67.08\%. What the sweep rules out is the reading that a randomly initialised model \emph{cannot} reach the pre-trained backbone's accuracy under this tokenizer: three from-scratch arms do, and the deepest exceeds it.

\subsection{Revised Interpretation of RC Test-Time Augmentation}

Section~\ref{sec:exp-rc-tta} reported that RC TTA is worth $+0.78$~pp to NT-Genus and $+0.08$~pp to the single-layer from-scratch model, and attributed the difference to strand biases acquired during pre-training. The depth sweep lets that interpretation be tested rather than assumed, and it survives only in part.

RC TTA remains worth approximately zero to KmerFormer \emph{at every depth}, not only at one layer, so the near-zero gain is not a consequence of limited capacity. Two further controls locate the mechanism more precisely. Ablating reverse-complement augmentation during \emph{training} moves 5M-read accuracy by 0.13~pp, inside the run-to-run spread, so the model does not depend on the augmentation to acquire strand invariance. And at $k=13$ the size of the RC-TTA gain tracks the \emph{vocabulary} rather than the architecture: an arm with a canonical vocabulary---one in which a $k$-mer and its reverse complement share an entry---gains $+0.01$~pp, because test-time augmentation is then arithmetically a no-op, whereas a non-canonical hashed vocabulary gains $+6.71$~pp, because the two strands hash to different buckets (Section~\ref{sec:exp-thirteenmer}).

The revised statement is therefore narrower than the original: RC TTA corrects a strand asymmetry that a model has been \emph{allowed to acquire}, and whether a model acquires one is governed by whether its tokenization is strand-symmetric and whether its training exposed it to both strands. NT-v2, pre-trained on single-strand genomic sequence with a non-canonical 6-mer vocabulary, has such an asymmetry; a from-scratch model trained with RC augmentation does not, at any depth.

\subsection{Efficiency}

The efficiency argument is strengthened rather than weakened by the corrected attribution, because the from-scratch arm is now the more accurate one as well as the cheaper one. At 29 layers KmerFormer uses 6.45M parameters against NT-v2's 500M, infers $7.6\times$ faster on the same GPU and trains $4.5\times$ faster in $11\times$ less memory; at one layer it is $84\times$ faster at inference on 27~MiB of peak memory. Full throughput and peak-memory measurements for every arm in this thesis are in Section~\ref{sec:exp-resources}.

\paragraph{Effect of tokenization (MetaTransformer architecture held constant).}
Tokenization design has a far larger effect than pre-training. Two sub-effects compound:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stride (overlapping vs.\ non-overlapping)}: Across all $k$ values, overlapping stride$=$1 dominates non-overlapping stride$=k$. The gap widens dramatically with $k$: $+1.87$~pp for $k=6$, $+38.17$~pp for $k=12$, and $+60.12$~pp for $k=13$. Non-overlapping tokenization with large $k$ reduces each 150~bp read to as few as 11 tokens, discarding most sequence context.

Longer tokens are taxonomically more specific---hexamers are shared across distant taxa, while 12-mers and 13-mers increasingly identify individual lineages.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Decomposition from a common baseline.}
A decomposition is only as good as the comparisons it is built from, so we restrict it to changes that move one factor inside one codebase. Two such comparisons are available and they point the same way. Within MetaTransformer's own architecture and training recipe, changing only the tokenizer is worth up to $+40.42$~pp (Table~\ref{tab:pretraining-tokenization-decomp}). Within ours, changing only the depth at a fixed 6-mer tokenizer is worth $+15.60$~pp (Section~\ref{sec:exp-shallow}). Tokenization is therefore the larger of the two factors by roughly $2.6\times$, and both are measured without crossing a codebase boundary.

That row is withdrawn. It was not a single-factor change, and the depth sweep shows that what it mostly measured was depth. The one cross-codebase comparison that is close to like-for-like runs the other way and is worth recording: at one layer and the same non-overlapping 6-mer tokenizer, KmerFormer reaches 53.92\% against MetaTransformer's 47.00\%, so $+6.92$~pp separates two single-layer from-scratch models that differ only in implementation and recipe---pre-norm rather than post-norm, learned rather than sinusoidal positions, an attention-pooling head rather than an unmasked mean.

\paragraph{The bottleneck is representational: a train-fit ceiling.}
The decomposition above measures held-out accuracy, which conflates representational capacity with generalisation. Table~\ref{tab:train-fit} reports training and validation accuracy at the largest (250M-read) scale. The NT-v2 6-mer models plateau at $63$--$66\%$ \emph{on the training set itself}, essentially equal to their validation accuracy (train--val gap ${\le}1$~pp)---they are not overfitting, they simply cannot fit. By contrast, MetaTransformer with overlapping 13-mer tokenisation fits the same task to $98.9\%$ training accuracy ($97.5\%$ validation).

The implication is decisive: with non-overlapping 6-mer tokenisation, reads from different genera are mapped to token sequences too similar to be separated, so no amount of additional capacity, data, or training can raise accuracy---the ceiling is imposed before learning begins, by the tokenizer. Overlapping 13-mers preserve enough sequence detail that the \emph{same model family} fits the data almost perfectly. This train-fit ceiling is the strongest single piece of evidence that tokenization, rather than backbone capacity or data volume, is the operative bottleneck for 6-mer GFMs on this task.

Three things follow. NT-v2's \emph{non-overlapping} 6-mer beats DNABERT's
\emph{overlapping} one by 1.27~pp---the opposite of the from-scratch result, where
overlapping stride was worth $+1.87$~pp at $k=6$ and $+60.12$ at $k=13$---so a larger
backbone (499M against 86M) with a prokaryote-containing corpus outweighs the stride
advantage at this budget. DNABERT at 5M reads (61.78\%) also beats MetaTransformer at
50M reads with the \emph{same} overlapping 6-mer tokenizer (48.87\%), on a tenth of the
data: the clearest single demonstration in this thesis that pre-training buys data
efficiency, and consistent with the depth-matched crossover of
Section~\ref{sec:exp-lowdata}. And DNABERT-2's BPE (58.88\%) trails both fixed-$k$
models at a comparable parameter count, which suggests a frequency-derived vocabulary
fragments the taxonomically informative motifs that fixed-length $k$-mers keep intact.

\paragraph{Methodological note on training equivalence.}
The three models share the learning rate ($5\times10^{-4}$ head/LoRA,
$3\times10^{-5}$ backbone), warmup ratio, cosine schedule and epoch count, but differ
in batch size (32, 128, 256) because their sequence lengths differ (25, 145 and
${\sim}34$ tokens per read). The comparison is therefore epoch-based---each model sees
the data 30 times---rather than step-matched, so the gradient-update counts differ
(453K, 117K, 59K). The ranking is unlikely to be an artefact of that: DNABERT lags by
only 1.27~pp despite receiving $3.9\times$ fewer updates than NT-v2, and DNABERT-2's
4.17~pp gap is consistent across every training stage.

\paragraph{Sample-level evaluation of the three GFM backbones (5M scale).}
Read-level accuracy alone does not capture how a backbone choice propagates to the sample-level abundance and detection metrics that matter in practice.
We therefore apply the sample-level evaluation protocol (5M validation pool, 50K reads per sample, 100 partition samples; Section~\ref{sec:exp-sample}) to the RC-TTA predictions of all three pre-trained backbones at 5M scale.
Table~\ref{tab:backbone-tokenization-5M} reports the results.

Two patterns emerge.
First, abundance estimation (Pearson $r$) saturates at $\sim$0.99 for all three backbones, consistent with the genus-level finding that relative-abundance correlation is forgiving at low task granularity (Section~\ref{sec:sample-mt-comparison})---the read-accuracy ranking (NT-Genus $>$ DNABERT $>$ DNABERT-2) does not separate the backbones on Pearson $r$.
Second, the discriminating metrics are Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and binary detection: DNABERT-2 is clearly worst on both (Bray-Curtis 0.141 vs.\ 0.112--0.113; ROC AUC 0.640 vs.\ 0.666--0.672; sensitivity at 95\% specificity 11.6\% vs.\ 14.2--14.5\%), mirroring its lower read accuracy and reinforcing that BPE tokenisation is the weakest of the three for this task.
NT-Genus and DNABERT are nearly indistinguishable at the sample level, with NT-Genus marginally ahead on Bray-Curtis and sensitivity and DNABERT marginally ahead on ROC AUC.

\paragraph{The backbone comparison was not repeated at 50M, and why.}
The 5M comparison above is the compute-matched baseline that selected NT-v2, and it
is the only budget at which all three backbones were trained. Repeating it at 50M was
started and not completed: DNABERT-2 (BPE, ${\sim}34$ tokens/read) trains at
${\sim}2.8$~h/epoch, but DNABERT (overlapping 6-mer, 145 tokens/read) costs
${\sim}11.7$~h/epoch---four to five times NT-v2's per-epoch cost, from its $5\times$
longer token sequences---so a full 30-epoch run needs about 14.6 days of GPU time
across repeated SLURM resumes, which the available allocation did not cover. The
ranking at 5M is therefore the one this thesis reports, and the 50M comparison remains
open. That cost asymmetry is itself a finding worth stating: an overlapping-$k$-mer
backbone carries a sequence-length penalty that compounds with data scale, which is a
practical consideration in backbone selection independent of accuracy.

\paragraph{Implication for NT-v2.}
NT-v2 is constrained to 6-mer tokenization by its pre-training design. The ablation results show that this constraint is the primary limitation of our approach: switching to overlapping 13-mer tokenization on the same data yields a $+17.90$~pp improvement over the best 6-mer arm without any pre-training, and Section~\ref{sec:exp-thirteenmer} reproduces the effect inside our own codebase, where an exact 13-mer vocabulary reaches 91.15\%. A genomic foundation model pre-trained with overlapping large-$k$ tokenization on diverse microbial genomes would combine the discriminative power of longer k-mers with the representational quality of large-scale pre-training. No such model currently exists publicly.

\section{KmerFormer at $k=13$: Exact and Hashed Vocabularies}
\label{sec:exp-thirteenmer}

The tokenization ablation of Section~\ref{sec:exp-tokenization} establishes that a long overlapping $k$-mer is worth tens of percentage points, but it establishes it inside \emph{MetaTransformer's} codebase. That leaves an alternative reading open: perhaps what wins is not the tokenizer but the particular implementation that happens to carry it. Settling that requires training a long-$k$ arm in our own code, under our own recipe, on the same reads---which is what this section does.

Doing so is not merely a matter of changing a configuration flag, and the obstacle is instructive.

\subsection{The Vocabulary Is the Model}

At $k=13$ the training pool realises 33{,}545{,}099 distinct canonical 13-mers. At an embedding width of $d{=}64$ that is an 8~GiB parameter table, and 24~GiB once a sparse Adam variant materialises its two dense moment buffers---beyond a 24~GiB card, and the reason this experiment could not simply be run on the primary pipeline. Two consequences follow that are properties of long-$k$ models in general rather than of ours.

First, \textbf{the embedding table is the model}. For the single-layer configuration, 99.99\% of the 2.15~B parameters are the lookup table; encoder, pooling and classifier together are 187{,}704. A long-$k$ model is a very large lookup table with a very small network on top, which is the same accounting point already made for MetaTransformer in Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparison}, now confirmed from the inside.

Second, \textbf{an enumerated table does not train under data parallelism}. The exact vocabulary requires sparse gradients, and torch's DDP reducer rejects them, so the exact arms are confined to a single device while the hashed arms are not.

We therefore also train a \emph{hashed} variant, which maps each 13-mer through a MurmurHash3 64-bit finalizer with seed $s=42$ into $B=2^{22}=4{,}194{,}304$ buckets. At $d{=}128$ that is a 2~GiB table in place of an 8~GiB one, it trains under DDP, and---unlike the exact canonical vocabulary---it is not reverse-complement invariant, which turns out to matter for RC TTA.

\paragraph{The tokenizer, not the implementation.} An exact 13-mer vocabulary in our own code reaches 91.15\%, so the long-$k$ advantage reproduces across two independent implementations. We report the 3.68-point margin over MetaTransformer without resting on it: the two arms are matched on tokenizer, vocabulary, width, depth and data, and a reconstruction of MetaTransformer inside our codebase that is parameter-identical above the embedding table still leaves 2.70 of those points unexplained. Parameter identity is not behavioural identity, and a cross-codebase margin can be bounded but not attributed.

\paragraph{Depth reverses at $k=13$.} At $k=6$ depth was worth 15.60~pp (Section~\ref{sec:exp-shallow}); here it is harmful. One layer beats sixteen by 5.52 points with an exact vocabulary (91.15\% against 85.63\%) and by 2.97 with a hashed one (86.80\% against 83.83\%). Both vocabularies agree on the sign, so the reversal belongs to the tokenization rather than to one implementation of it. The interpretation is that a 13-mer table already stores the discriminative information; depth then adds capacity to memorise rather than to generalise.

\paragraph{Hashing costs 6.37 points and three quarters of the memory.} At matched width and depth, hashing costs 6.37 points ($d64$ exact 85.63\% against $d64$ hashed 79.26\%). Doubling the width to $d128$ recovers most of it, reaching 83.83\% on a 2~GiB table rather than an 8~GiB one. The hashed vocabulary is therefore best read as a capability rather than an accuracy result: it makes an overlapping 13-mer model trainable on hardware that cannot hold an enumerated table, and trainable under data parallelism at all.

\paragraph{It is $k$-mer length, not token count.} Switching the 6-mer tokenizer from non-overlapping to stride-1 turns 25 tokens into 146 without adding any information---the read is still 150~bp---and \emph{lowers} accuracy by 5.19 points (62.75\% against 67.94\%) at four times the training memory. Longer $k$-mers help; more tokens of the same $k$ do not.

\paragraph{RC TTA follows the vocabulary.} The exact arm uses a canonical vocabulary, in which a 13-mer and its reverse complement share an entry, so test-time augmentation is arithmetically a no-op and is measured at $+0.01$~pp. The hashed arm is not canonical---the two strands land in different buckets---and gains $+6.71$~pp. This is the control referred to in Section~\ref{sec:exp-shallow}: the size of the RC-TTA benefit is set by whether the tokenization is strand-symmetric, not by whether the model was pre-trained.

\section{Generalization Beyond the Reference Catalogue}
\label{sec:exp-offcat}

Every number reported so far is measured on reads simulated from genomes the reference catalogue contains. That is the regime in which a 13-mer model reaches 98.7\% at 250M reads (Section~\ref{sec:exp-scaling}), and Section~\ref{sec:exp-scaling} already cautions that such a figure approaches a lookup over known genomes rather than a measure of generalization. This section replaces that caution with a measurement.

\subsection{Two Off-Catalogue Pools}

\textbf{Track~A} simulates reads from RefSeq genomes absent from the training reference: 595{,}000 reads reduced to 580{,}000 over 116 genera after removing three accessions that overlap the training catalogue. It guarantees unseen \emph{assemblies} but mixes strains of seen species with genuinely novel species under one number.

\textbf{Track~B} is an ANI-stratified ladder built to separate those cases rather than average over them. Genomes are placed on three rungs by skani average nucleotide identity to the closest reference at the 95\% species boundary used by GTDB~\cite{Parks2022}: \textsc{strain} (a different strain of a species the reference holds), \textsc{species} (a different species of a represented genus), and \textsc{far} (no reference genome within reach). The same fourteen genera are present at every rung, so a line drawn through them measures distance and not class count, and chance is 7.1\% at all three. The label space is unchanged throughout: every read comes from a genome whose genus is one of the 120 trained on, so the task remains a 120-way decision with a correct answer available.

\subsection{The Ordering Inverts With Distance}

On Track~A the two tokenizations are level: 27.44\% for the 29-layer 6-mer KmerFormer against 27.05\% for the exact 13-mer arm, from closed-set figures of 69.52\% and 91.15\%. Both lose roughly two thirds of their accuracy, and the 24-point closed-set gap between them disappears entirely.

Track~B shows why, by separating the distances Track~A averages (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). On a different strain of a catalogued species the closed-set ordering still holds: MetaTransformer's 13-mer arm at 250M reads reaches 78.12\% against 45.87\% for KmerFormer at $k=6$. One rung out they are level (46.96\% against 45.77\%). At the far rung the ordering has inverted: 22.37\% against 11.07\%, an 11.3-point lead for the \emph{shorter} $k$-mer.

Retention separates the two vocabularies with nothing in between: across all arms measured, the long-$k$ ones keep 13--22\% of their strain-rung accuracy at the far rung and the 6-mer ones keep 49--54\%. Between those bands the architectures are as different as this thesis contains---one to twenty-nine encoder layers, exact and hashed vocabularies, 0.9M to 500M parameters, and in one case no neural network at all---and none of it moves an arm out of the band its tokenizer puts it in.

Two checks guard against reading this as an artefact of the ratio. The denominator is \emph{lower} for the 6-mer arms, which would inflate their retention if it were doing the work; but retention does not track the first rung inside either band, and where the starting points cross the tokenizer is still what retention follows---the hashed single-layer arm begins above the 29-layer 6-mer arm (50.03\% against 45.87\%) and ends far below it (6.57\% against 22.37\%). And retention describes the shape of a curve, not the worth of a model: the 6-mer naive Bayes retains the most of anything here, 54\%, by starting at 22.31\%. What recommends the 29-layer 6-mer model is that it does both---the highest first rung of any 6-mer arm and a retention that leaves it highest at the last.

\subsection{Depth Keeps Paying Off the Catalogue}

The depth series of Section~\ref{sec:exp-shallow} rises monotonically at every distance, including the far rung: 12.01\%, 20.10\%, 21.27\% and 22.37\% at 1, 8, 16 and 29 layers. Matched to the depths the 13-mer arms were trained at, sixteen layers over one is worth 16.91, 16.03 and 9.26 points at the strain, species and far rungs. Both 13-mer vocabularies do the reverse at all three rungs, and not one of those six differences carries the sign of the three 6-mer ones.

This is the sharpest form of the thesis's central claim. Depth is not a substitute for tokenization and tokenization is not a substitute for depth: which of them pays, and whether it pays at all, is decided by the tokenizer, and the answer is opposite at $k=6$ and $k=13$.

\subsection{The Same Crossover Without a Classifier}

The conclusion does not depend on any decision rule. For each model, take the pooled vector it hands to its own classifier and measure genus-neighbour purity---the fraction of a read's twenty nearest neighbours under cosine distance that carry its genus. With the same fourteen genera present at every rung, chance is 7.1\% in every panel.

At the strain rung the ordering is the closed set's: 73.0\% for our exact 13-mer arm and 70.2\% for MetaTransformer's, against 58.1\% for the 29-layer 6-mer model and 49.3\% for NT-v2. One rung out it has already turned over---the 6-mer arms at 60.9\% and 52.8\% against 55.3\% and 57.3\% for the 13-mer arms---and at the far rung the four sit in two bands with nothing between them: 44.8\% and 39.5\% against 21.3\% and 23.4\%. Retention of purity is 77\% and 80\% for the 6-mer arms against 29\% and 33\% for the long-$k$ ones, the same split the accuracies give, computed without a classifier in the path.

\subsection{What This Means for the Closed-Set Numbers}

Part of what a long $k$-mer buys on held-out reads from known genomes is \emph{recognition of those genomes} rather than transferable genus signal. The 98.7\% of Section~\ref{sec:exp-scaling} is therefore best read as an upper bound attained under complete reference coverage, and the caution attached to it there is now quantified: the same family of arms falls to 11.07\% where no reference genome is within reach, while a 6-mer model with 6.45M parameters against the 13-mer arm's 2.15\,B reaches 22.37\%.

Two practical consequences follow. Closed-set accuracy alone should not be used to rank methods for deployment, because the ranking does not survive the move off the reference; an ANI-graded test should accompany any headline number. And the choice of $k$ should follow the expected reference coverage of the study: long $k$-mers where the community is well catalogued, shorter ones where it is not.

\subsection{NT-Species: Final Results (Epoch 30)}
\label{sec:spv4-final}

NT-Species training converged at epoch~30 (April~2026), reaching a forward validation accuracy of \textbf{17.55\%} on the full 5M held-out set (1,535 classes). Appendix~\ref{app:species-metrics} gives the epoch-by-epoch progression and the complete metric profile at convergence.

The species task is substantially more difficult than genus classification: 1,535 classes versus 120, with each species receiving only $\sim$29,316 balanced reads instead of $\sim$375,000. The final 17.55\% accuracy, while modest in absolute terms, represents meaningful discrimination given the large class count (random baseline: 0.065\%, a relative lift of $\sim$270$\times$). Encouragingly, only 191 of 1,535 species have F1$=$0 under forward-only evaluation on the full 5M set, indicating that the long-tail problem is substantially less severe at full-scale evaluation than small-subset analysis suggests.

\paragraph{Extended evaluation modes.}
Full-scale evaluation of RC~TTA, oracle-genus, and top-$k$ genus routing on the 5M validation set was attempted but exceeded the 200~GB memory ceiling on the TWCC \texttt{normal} partition: the current \texttt{evaluate.py} retains all 5M $\times$ 1{,}535 logit and probability arrays ($\sim$60~GB of \texttt{float32} per pass) simultaneously when combining modes, which a streaming implementation could avoid. Because the genus-level RC~TTA benefit has already been quantified (+0.78~pp, Section~\ref{sec:exp-v9}) and the hierarchical routing analysis on the 100K subset (Section~\ref{sec:spv4-hierarchical-prelim}) already captures the key qualitative findings---oracle-genus gain of $+11.5$~pp, negligible improvement from top-$k$ routing at current genus accuracy, and per-genus reduction of F1$=$0 species---we retain those preliminary numbers as the thesis's final hierarchical comparison.

\subsection{Hierarchical Classification Pipeline}

With NT-Species converged, the top-$k$ genus routing evaluation mode (Section~\ref{sec:method-species-eval}) is combined with the NT-Genus model (67.07\% RC TTA accuracy). At this genus accuracy level, top-5 routing covers the correct genus in approximately 90.0\% of reads (from NT-Genus's Top-5 accuracy), providing a substantially smaller candidate set for species discrimination.

The hierarchical classification pipeline operates as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The genus classifier produces top-$k$ genus predictions (e.g., $k = 5$).
    \item The candidate species set is computed as the union of species belonging to these $k$ genera.
    \item The species classifier's logits are masked to this candidate set.
    \item The final species prediction is the argmax over the masked logits.
\end{enumerate}

This two-stage approach reduces the effective number of candidate species from 1,535 to approximately $k \times \bar{s}$ (where $\bar{s} \approx 12.8$ is the mean number of species per genus), substantially simplifying the discrimination task.

\subsection{Independent Test Set and Data-Leakage Audit}
\label{sec:spv4-data-leakage}

Hierarchical evaluation is sensitive to even small amounts of train--test contamination, because oracle and per-genus modes evaluate the model under restricted candidate sets where memorisation effects can be amplified.
We therefore performed an explicit data-leakage audit prior to reporting hierarchical results.

\textbf{Discovery.}
The 100K-read subset originally used as a held-out evaluation set was constructed by random sampling from the same FASTA file used for training, prior to applying the train/validation split.
Re-checking read identifiers showed that this subset shared more than 99\% of its reads with the 45M training set, rendering it effectively a training-set probe rather than an independent test set.

\textbf{Resolution.}
To obtain a genuinely independent test set under the same data-generation pipeline, we sampled 100K reads from the MetaTransformer validation reads (\texttt{val\_reads.fa}), generated by the same ART simulation protocol on the same 2,505 HGR-UMGS genomes but disjoint from our training stream.
This set has 0\% overlap with our 45M training pool and is used for all hierarchical-evaluation results below.

\textbf{Empirical impact.}
Re-evaluating the NT-Genus model on both sets yielded 66.6\% (overlapping 100K) vs.\ 66.1\% (independent 100K), a difference of 0.5~pp; the NT-Species flat baseline differs by less than 0.4~pp between the two sets.
We interpret this as evidence that the model has not memorised individual training reads---which would imply a much larger collapse on the disjoint set---but rather generalises at the read-content level.
Nevertheless, all hierarchical numbers reported below use the independent test set, and earlier preliminary numbers from the contaminated 100K subset are not reused.

\textbf{Scope of affected results.}
Genus-only training-set evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:exp-v9}) uses the standard 5M validation split and is not affected.
The data-leakage issue is specific to the 100K subset that had been re-used for hierarchical and oracle-routing evaluation; all hierarchical numbers in this section are computed on the independent test set.

\paragraph{The decomposition works; the router is what fails.}
Under perfect genus assignment the per-genus pipeline reaches 29.5\% Top-1 and macro
F1 0.283, above the flat NT-Species oracle (27.4\%, 0.252)---so genus-scoped
classifiers do discriminate species within a genus better than one classifier trained
across all 1{,}535 at once, and they do it with a \emph{frozen} backbone. Under
predicted routing at 66.1\% genus accuracy, the same pipeline reaches 15.6\%,
marginally \emph{below} the flat baseline (15.9\%). The asymmetry is structural: a
wrongly routed read is restricted to a candidate set that cannot contain its true
species, whereas the flat model's routing modes only reweight logits and move it by
$\pm0.1$~pp. The 13.9-point gap between predicted and oracle routing is therefore the
price of the router, and it is the quantity to improve.

\paragraph{The oracle ceiling is set by the tokenizer, not the architecture.}
Both oracle configurations land in the high 20s (27.4--29.5\%), while the MT 13-mer
flat model reaches \textbf{49.74\%} on the same test set with no routing at all---
$+20.2$~pp above the best NT-v2 result under \emph{any} routing mode. No routing
architecture recovers that margin inside the 6-mer framework.

\paragraph{Masking helps only above a router-quality threshold.}
Per-genus logit masking with the 87.5\% MT 13-mer router raises species Top-1 by
$+1.12$~pp (49.74\% $\to$ 50.86\%); the same masking with the 48.9\% MT 6-mer router
\emph{lowers} it by 2.81~pp (9.22\% $\to$ 6.41\%). With the NT-v2 pair the same
bidirectional pattern appears ($+12.9$~pp under oracle routing, $-1.2$ under
predicted). Masking is beneficial exactly when the router removes more wrong
candidates than correct ones, and the crossover lies between the 66.1\% router that
fails and the 87.5\% one that succeeds.

\subsection{Per-Genus Model Analysis}
\label{sec:spv4-per-genus}

The 81 per-genus classifiers were trained on the full 50M balanced dataset (approximately 32,600 reads per species within each genus), with the NT-v2 backbone frozen and only the attention-pooling head trained.
39 single-species genera require no classifier and are assigned deterministically.
Per-genus model accuracy depends strongly on genus size: genera with $\leq$5~species average $>$80\% accuracy, while those with $>$50~species average $<$20\%.
The overall mean validation accuracy is 48.3\% (median 44.2\%).

Performance is dominated by four large genera that collectively concentrate the majority of the species:
Collinsella (349 species, 1.3\% val acc), Clostridium (191 species, 19.9\%), Prevotella (91 species, 26.6\%), and Bacteroides (79 species, 15.9\%).
These genera alone account for a disproportionate share of the per-genus oracle gap relative to the full-scale NT-Species model.

To address this bottleneck, we investigate whether inserting a subgenus routing tier between the genus model and per-genus classifiers can reduce the effective class count and improve species Top-1 accuracy.

\subsection{Subgenus Routing: A Negative Result}
\label{sec:subgenus-routing}

The hardest genera---Collinsella (349~species), Clostridium (191), Prevotella (91)
and Bacteroides (79)---expose each per-genus classifier to hundreds of closely
related species at once. If those could be partitioned into subgroups of 25--30
species, intra-classifier confusion should fall. We tested this by extracting NT-v2
embeddings for training reads from the five largest genera, partitioning each with
$K$-means at $K = \lceil n_g / 27 \rceil$, and training a dedicated attention-pooling
head per subgroup on a frozen backbone, exactly as for the per-genus models.

The partition is not supported by the representation. For Collinsella the
species-level embedding clusters have a silhouette coefficient of $0.03$---near-random
structure---and a UMAP projection of the 349-species space is a continuous blob with
no visible per-species or per-cluster separation. NT-v2's non-overlapping 6-mer
representations simply do not separate species within a genus.

The routing results follow (Table~\ref{tab:subgenus-routing}). Hard routing, which
assigns each read to its highest-scoring subgroup, lowers Top-1 from 14.7\% to 13.2\%
under predicted genus routing and from 27.8\% to 25.6\% under oracle routing; soft
routing, which combines subgroup logits by confidence, recovers only part of that
(13.6\% and 26.1\%) and remains below the baseline in both conditions.

The failure follows from the embedding analysis rather than from the routing design:
a partition built on representations that do not separate species adds a second
source of routing error while removing no confusion. The bottleneck is
representational, so within-genus discrimination needs a better sequence
representation---an overlapping long-$k$ tokenizer---and not a deeper routing
hierarchy.

\section{Sample-Level Application Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp-sample}

Per-read classification accuracy captures only one dimension of practical utility.
In metagenomics, classifiers are applied to entire sequenced specimens---collections of reads whose aggregate predicted composition is used to answer ecological and clinical questions: which organisms are present, and at what relative abundance?
This section evaluates the NT-Genus model (67.07\% RC~TTA per-read accuracy) under two sample-level metrics: relative abundance estimation and binary genus detection.

The choice of evaluation metrics follows the conventions established by community-wide benchmarks of metagenomic profilers.
The Critical Assessment of Metagenome Interpretation (CAMI) challenges~\cite{Sczyrba2017,Meyer2022cami2} adopt the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ and the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity~\cite{BrayCurtis1957} as primary profile-level metrics; the abundance-estimation benchmark of Bracken~\cite{Lu2017}, together with the comprehensive classifier benchmark of McIntyre et al.~\cite{McIntyre2017}, additionally adopts the Pearson correlation coefficient $r$ between predicted and true per-taxon relative abundance.
The three metrics are complementary: Pearson $r$ measures how well predicted abundances track true abundances in absolute magnitude, Spearman $\rho$ measures whether the model preserves the rank ordering of taxa irrespective of magnitude, and Bray-Curtis quantifies the per-taxon $L_1$ error normalised to $[0, 1]$.
A model can attain high Spearman $\rho$ (correctly ranking taxa) while still incurring substantial Bray-Curtis dissimilarity from systematic over- or under-estimation; Pearson $r$ is sensitive to outlier taxa that dominate abundance.
Both Pearson $r$ and Spearman $\rho$ are reported throughout this section to provide complete information about correlation structure, while Bray-Curtis serves as the magnitude-aware $\beta$-diversity index from quantitative ecology widely used in microbiome studies.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Evaluations are conducted on the 5M-read validation set (120 genera, balanced).
Two types of synthetic metagenomic samples are constructed from the already-computed RC~TTA predictions (Section~\ref{sec:exp-v9}); no GPU inference is required.

\textbf{Random-partition samples} (abundance estimation): 100 non-overlapping samples of 50,000 reads each are formed by random permutation of the validation set.
All 120 genera are present in each sample.
Because the training data are balanced at the species level (each of the 1,535 species contributes an equal number of reads), the expected abundance of genus $g$ across all samples is $n_g / 1{,}535$, where $n_g$ is its species count in the reference database; genera with more member species are proportionally more abundant.
Relative abundance estimation is evaluated by comparing the true fraction $p_g = (\text{true reads of genus } g) / 50\,000$ against the predicted fraction $\hat{p}_g = (\text{predicted reads of genus } g) / 50\,000$.

\textbf{Sparse-community samples} (binary detection): 200 samples of 50,000 reads are constructed such that each sample contains reads from exactly 60 of the 120 genera (selected uniformly at random per sample).
The 60 absent genera serve as true negatives, enabling rigorous specificity measurement.

\label{sec:sample-abundance}

Despite a 33\% per-read error rate the predicted community composition tracks the true
one at Pearson $r = 0.993$, with Bray--Curtis dissimilarity 0.094: misclassified reads
scatter broadly across genera, so the errors largely cancel once reads are aggregated
into a profile. The residuals are not quite symmetric---high-abundance genera are
slightly overestimated---which is classifier bias rather than a data-generation
artefact. Reads misclassified away from rare genera fall disproportionately into the
decision regions of common genera, which have broader learned representations from
their larger species membership; and because the ART schedule is coverage-normalised
(Section~\ref{sec:method-data}) and species-balanced subsampling equalises read counts
before evaluation, the true profile depends only on species-per-genus, not on genome
length.

One structural property limits what the number means. All 100 random-partition samples
are drawn from the same species-balanced validation set, so every sample shares the
same \emph{expected} genus profile---genus $g$ always contributes $n_g/1{,}535$ of the
reads. The $r = 0.993$ therefore measures how well the classifier tracks one fixed
profile under binomial sampling noise, not how it would generalise to communities that
genuinely differ in composition. The sparse-community analysis below, where each sample
draws a different random subset of 60 genera, imposes real presence/absence variation
and is the more realistic test.

Every genus at or above 1\% relative abundance is detected, which follows from the
arithmetic: 1\% of a 50{,}000-read sample is 500 reads, and at 67\% recall roughly 335
are assigned correctly, so a false negative is essentially impossible. Even at
0.01--0.1\% abundance---5 to 50 reads per genus per sample---sensitivity is 93.9\%.

Threshold-invariant performance is worse. Treating a genus as truly present exactly
when the sparse-community construction included it, and scoring it by its predicted
relative abundance, sweeping that score gives ROC AUC 0.705; sensitivity is 17.2\% at
95\% specificity, 30.9\% at 90\% and 46.2\% at 80\%. A noise floor is the reason. At a
33\% error rate a truly \emph{absent} genus still collects about
$50{,}000 \times 0.33 / 120 \approx 138$ misclassified reads, a spurious abundance of
${\sim}0.28\%$, so reaching 95\% specificity needs a threshold above 1.49\% predicted
abundance---which suppresses exactly the low-abundance genera detection is for.

That is the asymmetry between the two sample-level tasks. Abundance estimation works
because errors out of a genus are largely cancelled by errors into it. Detection
requires separating an absent genus holding ${\sim}138$ spurious reads from a present
but rare one holding only slightly more, and at 67\% per-read accuracy those are not
well separated. Improving it needs either higher per-read accuracy, which lowers the
floor, or a calibrated test for presence above background; we leave the latter to
future work.

\subsection{Species-Level Sample Evaluation}
\label{sec:sample-species}

The genus-level evaluation above establishes that aggregate abundance profiles are robust to per-read misclassification when the task granularity is low (120 classes).
We now examine whether this robustness extends to species-level classification (1,535 classes), using the independent 100K test set introduced in Section~\ref{sec:spv4-hierarchical-prelim}.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
Because the 100K test set contains only $\sim$65 reads per species on average, sample parameters are reduced relative to the genus evaluation: reads per sample = 1,000 (100 non-overlapping partition samples); sparse-community samples use 50 genera present per sample.
Five models are evaluated: the NT-Species flat classifier (Section~\ref{sec:spv4-final}), the MT 13-mer flat and hierarchical species models, and the MT 6-mer flat and hierarchical species models (Section~\ref{sec:spv4-hierarchical-prelim}).

\paragraph{A practical utility threshold.}
Table~\ref{tab:sample-species-comparison} in Appendix~\ref{app:sample-tables} reveals a large performance gap between models with read accuracy below $\sim$20\% and those above $\sim$50\%.
The NT-Species flat classifier achieves 17.8\% per-read accuracy with Pearson $r = 0.135$ and Spearman $\rho = 0.144$---low but non-zero abundance correlation---while the MT 13-mer flat classifier achieves 53.7\%, Pearson $r = 0.503$, and Spearman $\rho = 0.508$.
The 3.7$\times$ gap in Pearson $r$ despite a 36~pp gap in read accuracy reflects highly non-linear signal-to-noise behaviour at the species level.
With 1,535 species and 1,000 reads per sample, each species contributes on average only $\sim$0.65 reads to a sample.
At 17.8\% accuracy, 82.2\% of reads are misclassified; these generate approximately $1{,}000 \times 0.822 / 1{,}534 \approx 0.54$ spurious predicted reads per species---a noise floor comparable to the expected signal.
This near-unit signal-to-noise ratio constrains Pearson $r$ to $\approx 0.14$, well below the useful range for abundance profiling.
At 53.7\% accuracy, approximately half the reads are correctly assigned; the reduced noise allows Pearson $r$ to rise to 0.503, crossing the practical utility threshold.

This observation establishes a practical threshold for metagenomic utility at the species level: approximately 40--50\% per-read accuracy is required before sample-level abundance profiles become meaningful.
Within the NT-v2 framework (maximum 15.9\% species Top-1 with 6-mer tokenisation), this threshold cannot be reached without a fundamental change to the tokenisation scheme.
The MT 13-mer result (53.7\%) crosses this threshold, demonstrating that overlapping long-$k$ tokenisation is not merely a read-accuracy optimisation but a prerequisite for species-level abundance estimation.

\paragraph{Hierarchical masking helps only above a router-quality threshold.}
Two router--model pairs could be scored at the sample level on this test set: the
66.1\% NT-Genus router applied to NT-Species, and the 48.9\% MT 6-mer router applied
to the MT 6-mer species model. (The MT 13-mer hierarchical checkpoint on Taiwania-2
was corrupted---training halted at batch 10{,}000 with validation loss 9.29 and never
converged---so its read-level numbers in
Section~\ref{sec:spv4-hierarchical-prelim} stand but no sample-level metrics aligned
to this pool exist.)

Both available pairs degrade under masking, and the worse router degrades more. With
the 66.1\% router, read accuracy falls 17.8\% $\to$ 15.8\%, Pearson $r$ 0.135 $\to$
0.106, Bray--Curtis 0.589 $\to$ 0.608 and sensitivity at 95\% specificity 55.2\% $\to$
53.0\%; only ROC AUC moves the other way, by $+0.002$. With the 48.9\% router the
losses roughly double: 9.0\% $\to$ 6.4\% read accuracy, $r$ 0.066 $\to$ 0.041, AUC
0.692 $\to$ 0.639. The mechanism is direct: when a read is routed to the wrong genus,
masking removes its true species from the candidate set and guarantees the error.
Together with the $+1.12$~pp that the 87.5\% router buys at read level
(Section~\ref{sec:spv4-hierarchical-prelim}), the three points are monotonic in
router quality and put the crossover at roughly 80--85\% genus accuracy.

\paragraph{Every 6-mer model sits below the utility threshold.}
The MT 6-mer flat model reaches 9.0\% per-read accuracy and $r = 0.066$, and all four
6-mer-based species models---NT-Species flat and hierarchical, MT 6-mer flat and
hierarchical---fall short of the 40--50\% accuracy that species-level abundance
requires. Binary detection separates them in the expected order (NT-Species 0.794 and
0.796 against MT 6-mer's 0.639--0.692) and all four sit far below the MT 13-mer flat
ceiling of 0.973.

\paragraph{The per-genus pipeline is router-bound, and its oracle ceiling is
tokenizer-bound.} With predicted routing at 66.0\%, the two-stage NT-v2 pipeline is
worse than the flat classifier on every sample-level metric (read accuracy 15.6\%
against 17.8\%, $r$ 0.083 against 0.135, Bray--Curtis 0.625 against 0.589, AUC 0.789
against 0.794). Under oracle routing it is the best NT-v2 configuration on every
metric: 29.5\% read accuracy, $r = 0.159$, AUC 0.842, sensitivity at 95\% specificity
65.2\%. The 13.9-point gap between predicted and oracle is the price of the router;
the 24.2-point gap that remains between the oracle and MT 13-mer flat (53.7\%) is the
price of the tokenizer, and only the first is an architectural problem.

\paragraph{Kraken\,2 is the strongest baseline on in-database reads, and abstention is
why.} Against a custom Kraken\,2 database ($k = 35$, minimiser $l = 31$) built from
the same 2{,}505 UMGS$+$HGR genomes and run on the same 100K test set, Kraken\,2
reaches 66.2\% per-read accuracy with $r = 0.727$, Bray--Curtis 0.218 and AUC 0.925---
above the best neural species model on read accuracy ($+12$~pp), correlation
($+0.22$) and dissimilarity ($-0.13$). Simulated 150~bp reads are exact substrings of
the reference genomes, so exact 35-mer matching is close to the ideal method for this
pool. It commits to 70\% of reads and is 94.6\% accurate at species level and 99.3\%
at genus level on those; at genus level overall it reaches 69.5\%, $r = 0.844$,
Bray--Curtis 0.178, AUC 0.895. The one metric where MT 13-mer leads is species
detection AUC (0.973 against 0.925), which follows from the same abstention: the 30\%
of reads Kraken\,2 declines produce no detection signal, while a neural model returns
a posterior for every read.

\paragraph{A coverage-matched comparison, and what survives it.}
Our database covers 1{,}316 of the 1{,}535 test species; the remaining 219 are
GCF/RefSeq-sourced with no locally available assembly, so Kraken\,2 cannot classify
their 14{,}181 reads by construction. That is database coverage, not generalisation:
those species are in the neural models' training data. Restricting to the 85{,}819
in-DB reads (Table~\ref{tab:in-db-fair}, Appendix~\ref{app:sample-tables}) inverts the
ranking between the two taxonomic levels. At species level Kraken\,2 dominates
everything---77.18\% read accuracy and $r = 0.847$, more than 24~pp and 0.33 above MT
13-mer flat---on the strength of 99.33\% precision over the reads it commits to. At
genus level both neural models pass \emph{raw} Kraken\,2 on abundance ($r = 0.9993$
for MT 13-mer and 0.992 for NT-Genus against 0.823), and NT-Genus does so despite a
lower read accuracy (68.9\% against 77.7\%), because a uniform 30\% abstention
suppresses all 120 genus counts and deflates the correlation.

That advantage does not survive a correctly configured baseline. Applying
Bracken~\cite{Lu2017}, the standard re-estimation step of a Kraken\,2 pipeline, lifts
genus-level abundance to $r = 0.997$ and Bray--Curtis 0.060 (from $r = 0.845$ raw on
the same full report)---the same near-perfect range as the neural models. Against
Kraken\,2$+$Bracken the neural models hold \emph{no} sample-level abundance advantage,
and the apparent one was an artefact of comparing against uncorrected counts. What
does survive is detection, where Kraken\,2 leads decisively (AUC 0.966 and 93.5\%
sensitivity at 95\% specificity, against 0.905/66.5\% for MT 13-mer and 0.681/17.5\%
for NT-Genus, the last limited by the noise floor of
Section~\ref{sec:sample-detection}), and coverage, where it does not: Kraken\,2 scores
0\% on the 14\% of species without an assembly, while a neural classifier trains from
labelled reads and needs no per-species reference at all. Many MAGs of biological
interest have no high-quality assembly, so any reference-based method is structurally
bounded by assembly coverage in a way a learned one is not.

\section{Summary of Findings}

Three design factors were separated in this chapter, and they do not carry equal
weight. Tokenization is the largest: within MetaTransformer's own architecture,
changing only the tokenizer is worth up to $+40.42$~pp, and an exact 13-mer
vocabulary in our own codebase reaches 91.15\%. Encoder depth is the second, and its
sign depends on the tokenizer---$+15.60$~pp from 1 to 29 layers at $k=6$, and
negative at $k=13$, where one layer beats sixteen. Pre-training is worth what the
labelled-data budget makes it worth: approximately zero at 50M reads once depth is
matched, and $+8.9$~pp at 5M, on both catalogues.

Two results qualify the rest. Off the reference catalogue the ranking inverts with
distance, so closed-set accuracy measures recognition of catalogued genomes rather
than transferable signal. And at the sample level, per-read accuracy understates
utility for abundance profiling and overstates it for detection, so no single
accuracy column ranks these methods for deployment. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}
develops what follows from each.
